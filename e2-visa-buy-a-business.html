E-2 visa requirements when you're buying a business

The treaty investor visa has four tests, and a business-for-sale listing either passes them or it doesn't — before you spend on a business plan, an attorney, or a deposit. Here is what the tests are, what "substantial" means in practice, why buying an existing business is usually the stronger path, and how to screen a listing in an afternoon.

XP M&A Advisory · Reviewed September 2026 · 11 minute read · Not legal advice

Read this first. This page explains the E-2 tests as they apply to choosing and screening a business. It is not immigration advice, and it does not replace a licensed immigration attorney, who makes the actual call on your case. What it does is let you arrive at that attorney with a business that plausibly qualifies, instead of paying for a plan on one that never could.

What the E-2 is

The E-2 is a non-immigrant visa for nationals of countries that hold a qualifying treaty of commerce with the United States. It lets you enter to develop and direct a business you have invested in. It is renewable for as long as the business qualifies, in increments set by your country's treaty, and your spouse can work. It is not a path to permanent residence by itself — that takes a separate programme — but many investors hold E-2 status for years.

For most applicants the practical question is not eligibility in the abstract. It is: which business, at what price, structured how? That is a deal question before it is a legal one, and it is the part most buyers get in the wrong order.

The four tests a listing has to pass

Treaty nationality

You must be a national of a treaty country, and the business must be at least 50% owned by nationals of that country. The State Department maintains the list. If your country isn't on it, nothing else on this page applies — check this first.

Substantial, at-risk, irrevocably committed investment

The money must be yours, lawfully sourced, already committed to the business, and genuinely exposed to loss. Funds sitting in escrow pending visa approval generally do not count as committed — though a purchase agreement contingent only on the visa, with funds in escrow, is a recognised structure. "Substantial" is proportional, not a fixed number; see below.

Real and operating, not marginal

The business must be a real commercial enterprise producing goods or services, and it must not be marginal. Marginal means it generates only enough to support you and your family. To pass, it either produces income well beyond that, or has the capacity to make a significant economic contribution — in practice, jobs for US workers — within about five years.

Develop and direct

You must be in a position to control the business: at least 50% ownership, or operational control through a management position. A passive minority stake does not qualify. If you are buying with a partner, the ownership split matters.

What "substantial" actually means

There is no statutory minimum, which is the first thing every applicant wants and no attorney will give them. The test is proportionality: the investment must be substantial relative to the total cost of establishing or purchasing the enterprise. Consular officers apply a sliding scale — on a low-cost business they expect the investor to have put in nearly all of it; on an expensive one, a lower percentage can be acceptable.

In practice, buying an existing business in the $150,000 to $500,000 range with most of the price invested is a common and credible profile. Investments under about $100,000 draw scrutiny on both proportionality and marginality — not because there is a floor, but because a business that cheap is often one that cannot support a family or create jobs.

The number that matters is the ratio, not the dollar figure. $250,000 invested into a $260,000 business is stronger than $250,000 invested into a $900,000 business financed mostly with debt. Officers look at how much of the enterprise is your money at risk. This is also why heavy SBA financing — which is not available to most E-2 applicants anyway — and E-2 objectives pull in opposite directions. If you are a US citizen or permanent resident and the visa question doesn't apply, the financing path is different: see using an SBA loan to buy a business.

Why buying an existing business is usually the stronger path

A start-up has to prove it will be real, operating, and non-marginal, using projections. An existing business proves it already is, using tax returns. Three years of financials showing revenue, profit, and payroll do more work at a consular interview than any business plan.

The purchase also gives the investment a clear, documentable total cost — the price plus working capital — which makes the proportionality argument straightforward. And employees who come with the business satisfy the job-creation dimension of marginality on day one, rather than as a promise.

The trade-off is diligence. You are buying someone else's numbers, and the same add-backs that inflate a listing for an SBA buyer inflate it for you. If the real profit after normalisation only supports one family, the business is marginal regardless of what the broker's summary says. The deal analysis exists for exactly this: what the business actually earns, and whether that clears the bar.

The business plan

For an existing business, the historical financials carry most of the weight. Most attorneys still want a plan with five-year projections, because the officer is evaluating the enterprise under your ownership: how it grows, how many US workers it employs, and why it stays non-marginal. The plan should be grounded in the historical numbers rather than optimistic assumptions layered on top of them — officers have seen a great many hockey-stick projections and discount them accordingly.

A plan built on a deal analysis has a real advantage: the projections start from normalised earnings, not the broker's recast, and the job-creation narrative starts from the actual payroll.

Common mistakes in acquisitions

Buying too cheap. A $60,000 business rarely clears marginality and invites proportionality questions. The instinct to minimise risk works against the visa.

Financing most of the price. Debt reduces the share of the enterprise that is your money at risk. Some seller financing is workable; a structure where you have put in 20% and borrowed the rest generally is not.

Passive structures. Buying a rental property, a franchise you don't operate, or a minority stake. The visa is for developing and directing, and officers look for it.

Committing before checking nationality and ownership. A partner who isn't a treaty national can break the 50% requirement. Check the cap table before the letter of intent.

Undocumented funds. The source-of-funds file is often the longest part of the application. Start it before you start looking at businesses.

E-2 versus EB-5

They answer different questions. EB-5 is an immigrant visa — a path to a green card — requiring an investment of roughly $800,000 to $1.05 million and the creation of ten full-time jobs. E-2 is a business visa with no fixed minimum, faster, renewable indefinitely, and not a residency programme. Buyers in the $150,000 to $2 million range are almost always looking at E-2. Those who want permanent residence and have the capital look at EB-5, sometimes after years on E-2.

Questions buyers ask

What is the minimum investment for an E-2 visa?

There isn't a fixed one. The investment must be substantial in proportion to the total cost of the business. Existing businesses in the $150,000 to $500,000 range with most of the price invested are a common, credible profile; investments under about $100,000 face more scrutiny.

Can you buy an existing business for an E-2?

Yes, and it's often the stronger path. The business already satisfies real-and-operating, and its historical financials make marginality easier to demonstrate than projections.

What is the marginality test?

The business must generate more than a minimal living for you and your family, or have the capacity to create jobs for US workers within about five years. An existing business with staff and profit beyond a living usually satisfies it on its historical numbers.

Does an E-2 lead to a green card?

Not by itself. It's a non-immigrant visa, renewable while the business qualifies. Permanent residence takes a separate path such as EB-5.

Do I need a business plan?

For a new business, almost always. For an acquisition, the financials do most of the work, but most attorneys still want five-year projections under your ownership.
